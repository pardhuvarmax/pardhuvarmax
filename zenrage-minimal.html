Deployment & infrastructure

Challenge authoring stops at Docker; getting it live — and keeping it live — during a 24-to-36-hour competition window is its own job, across both seasons.

Season 1 is where this started: almost every deployment-class challenge in Siege of Troy — Break The Chain's lending/token/oracle/insurance stack, the NFT marketplace, Trusted Envoy's API surface — was containerized, deployed, and kept alive by hand during the live event, Dockerfiles and compose configs patched in place when something broke mid-competition rather than pulled and rebuilt from scratch. Hosting infrastructure came down again once the event closed; the writeups kept the live URLs as a record of what was actually running.

Season 2's insane-tier stack runs on Google Cloud — verified as a working host down to the kernel level, not just "it booted." Continuity Paradox needs a real kernel with eBPF and BTF support to attach a live syscall tracepoint, and can't run under a syscall-sandboxed runtime like gVisor or Kata; GCP was the actual target confirmed to meet that bar, including working around Intel TSX being disabled there rather than assuming it away.

Siege of Troy: writing Season 1

Eleven challenges built around one idea: the city did not fall to force, it fell to trust.

Season 1 of BreachPoint CTF, Siege of Troy, is where it started for me: eleven challenges across reverse engineering, cryptography, blockchain, binary exploitation, steganography and OSINT, with flags in the bpctf{...} format.

The theme

Troy didn't fall to force. It fell to an envoy carrying marks the city had trusted for generations. Almost every challenge in the season is a version of that idea: something the system assumes is safe, and the player proves it isn't.

What that looked like

  • Reverse engineering. Zigster Like A Trickster buries its key under math that cancels itself out. Dispatcher Of Deceit sends you down the wrong path on purpose, and the “fake” validation is the tell. Phantom Engine says “wrong” even for the right seed, because the seed calculation itself is unreliable.
  • Cryptography. Biased Stream looks noisy and “secure enough”, but the bias is measurable and the key falls out of it. BB84 But Lazy is a simulated quantum key distribution session with a subtle flaw: you play Eve and recover the key from the transcript alone. Rolling Silence hides a custom stream cipher inside a stripped, symbol-free x86_64 binary.
  • Binary exploitation and blockchain. The Rusty Frame leans on the fact that Rust's memory guarantees only hold where the program still believes they apply. NFT Marketplace is a dormant CREATE2-deployed contract from a 2014 experimental phase, and Break The Chain stacks a lending platform, a token, an oracle and an insurance protocol that each reinforce the next.
  • Steganography. Troy's Rabbit Hole hides JPEG-style frequency analysis inside an 8-bit grayscale PNG processed as non-overlapping 8×8 blocks, a container that was never JPEG at all.

Running it

Season 1 ran as a 24-hour event. Almost every deployment-class challenge was containerized, deployed and kept alive by hand, with Dockerfiles and compose configs patched in place when something broke mid-competition. Season 1 is where the infrastructure work started; Season 2 is covered in the cloud operations post.

The rule I kept

The line from the About section applies here too: the first thing you pull out will look exactly like the flag. It isn't.

Federation: writing Season 2

Ten harder challenges, built as chains where the last step has to be earned.

Season 2, Federation, has ten challenges across web, API, reverse engineering, IoT, OT, steganography and miscellaneous, worth 200 to 400 points each, with flags in the BREACH{...} format.

Chains, not puzzles

Solve chains stay in scope: no layer is there for padding, and the last step is usually gated on proving you actually understood the ones before it, not just brute-forcing your way past them.

Web and API

  • ChessNet. A public correspondence-chess bracket sits in front of eight unrelated internal services. Register, beat a real legal-move engine, and clear eight independent bugs.
  • Cold Rules. An internal support console triages tickets with an AI assistant. An obfuscated chatbot hides an SSRF, and the chain runs on into request smuggling.
  • Covert Opera. One address is given: a public deposition intake. The flag lives on a second, undisclosed origin, reachable only via a stray Link header, a CSS-only exfiltration channel under script-src 'none', and a web cache deception.
  • Legacy Federated Monopoly. A federated Apollo GraphQL gateway with introspection locked down and a legacy WebSocket client nobody finished retiring after the last migration.

Systems, hardware and the physical world

  • Continuity Paradox. One TCP port and nothing else. Earn local execution through a real exploit, then prove the true ordering of a disputed memory write against a live eBPF-observed syscall trace.
  • House of Silicon Chaos. A binary that genuinely executes and checks RDRAND. The RNG isn't the bug; the vulnerability is a chain of two failure-handling bugs that only engage when RDRAND, rarely, reports failure.
  • IronCold. Intercepted firmware for a propellant feed controller aboard an industrial solar tug: XOR obfuscation, weakened RSA, and a vault key generated fresh in RAM on every boot, across three flags.
  • Vergil's Verdict. Operational technology doesn't get to say “it looked fine on the HMI.” A verdict means proving the actual state of the process, not the state the panel is showing you.

Steganography and the rest

Rabbit Hole II: Singularity is a signal thirty-two years in transit and two files that come back clean from every tool you throw at them, because those tools are answering a different question than the one you are asking. The Dead Man's Tale is the medium-difficulty tale left behind for someone else to finish reading.

A note on hosting

Continuity Paradox has the strictest hosting requirements of the season. The details are in the cloud operations post.

Keeping a CTF alive: cloud deployment operations

From hand-patched Docker Compose in Season 1 to a verified Google Cloud host in Season 2.

Writing a challenge stops at Docker. Getting it live, and keeping it live, during a 24-to-36-hour competition window is its own job. I did the cloud deployment operations for BreachPoint CTF full-time in February and March 2026, in Hyderabad.

One stack, one command

Every hosted challenge is a Docker Compose stack that boots clean with one command. The flag is generated in memory at deploy time, so nothing is static and nothing is reused across instances.

Season 1: by hand

Almost every deployment-class challenge in Siege of Troy was containerized, deployed and kept alive by hand during the live event, with Dockerfiles and compose configs patched in place when something broke mid-competition rather than pulled and rebuilt from scratch. The hosting came down once the event closed; the writeups kept the live URLs as a record of what was actually running.

Season 2: a real kernel

Season 2's hardest stack runs on Google Cloud, verified as a working host down to the kernel level, not just “it booted.” Continuity Paradox needs a real kernel with eBPF and BTF support to attach a live syscall tracepoint, and it can't run under a syscall-sandboxed runtime like gVisor or Kata. GCP was the target confirmed to meet that bar, including working around Intel TSX being disabled there rather than assuming it away.

Guardrails

  • Privilege model. Scoped per challenge, not blanket-granted: cap_add: [BPF, PERFMON] instead of --privileged or CAP_SYS_ADMIN, then programmatically dropped from the bounding set before any player-reachable code runs.
  • Resource limits. Per-challenge mem_limit and cpus, tuned individually rather than a single shared default across the fleet.
  • Restart policy. unless-stopped where a clean respawn is just recovery; on-failure where the crash-and-respawn cycle is part of the intended exploit path.

Keeping the board honest

GitHub Actions recomputes the readiness dashboard and challenge log straight from each folder's own README on every push touching a challenge category, so the status board can't silently drift from what is actually in the repo. The same discipline extends into the event window itself: keeping instances up, isolated, and swapped or patched without disrupting a running scoreboard.
